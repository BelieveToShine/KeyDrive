❓ "Here is a messy service class. How would you decide what to refactor and which principle applies?"
👁 See it first
🎯 You know the five principles, DI, smells, refactoring, testing and when to stop. This is the practice ward: symptom → evidence → diagnosis → one change → prove it.
📖 Why did this need to exist?

Real tickets say “every promotion breaks something” or “tests take forever”, not “this violates OCP”. Each case starts the way a ticket does, using the same pictures as the lab: restaurant, power strip, birds, printer, light switch.

Every case is solved with the ladder: smell → read (who changes it, and why?) → find the bent principle → guess one change → make it small and safe → prove it with a test.

🧭 The ladder, SOLID edition
1 · SMELL ★

👃 What hurts?

A change that touches many files, a test that needs a database, a class nobody dares edit.

2 · READ ★

🔬 Who changes this, and why?

List the reasons the class changes and the things it depends on.

3 · FIND ★

🎯 The principle that is bent

One responsibility too many? A broken contract? A concrete dependency?

4 · GUESS ★

💡 One refactoring

Extract a class, introduce an interface, invert a dependency.

5 · CHANGE ★

🔧 One small, safe step

Keep tests green after every step.

6 · PROVE ★

✅ Re-measure

Fewer files touched per change; tests that need no database.

Ask in this order: what hurts (changing, testing, or understanding)? → who or what forces the change? → which principle does the evidence point to? → what is the smallest refactoring? → does it still pass the pinned test, and is the next change cheaper? → or is it better left alone?

🩺 Case 1

Every promotion edits one huge method

Symptom. Marketing asks for “buy 2 get 1 free”. The change touches OrderService and breaks free shipping.

Evidence.

  • OrderService is ≈ 300 lines; pricing alone is ≈ 120 lines of nested if/else.
  • Git history: 14 of the last 20 changes touched this file, for 4 different reasons.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. One class changes for many reasons (SRP) and the growing part is a conditional (OCP).

Fix. Extract pricing into its own class, then turn promotions into IDiscountRule classes. A new promotion is one new class.

📖 Where to review: pages 2 and 3.

🩺 Case 2

The loop that makes every bird fly crashes

Symptom. A zoo app calls Fly() on every bird. It works all week — then crashes on a penguin.

Evidence.

  • Penguin.Fly() contains throw new NotSupportedException().
  • The code compiled; the unit tests only used sparrows.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. A child that cannot keep its parent’s promise (Liskov). Bird promised something not every bird can do.

Fix. Bird only promises Eat(); Fly() moves into IFlyable; the loop takes IFlyables.

📖 Where to review: page 4.

🩺 Case 3

Tests need a real database and send real emails

Symptom. A one-line test takes ages and sometimes emails real customers.

Evidence.

  • OrderService.Place contains new SqlConnection(...) and new SmtpClient(...).
  • Its constructor takes nothing.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. The important class wires itself to one particular database and mail server, like a switch wired to one bulb (Dependency Inversion).

Fix. Introduce IOrderRepository and INotifier, receive them in the constructor, register them in Program.cs. Tests plug in fakes.

📖 Where to review: pages 6, 7 and 10.

🩺 Case 4

Adding a payment method means five edits

Symptom. Adding PayPal needs changes in checkout, refunds, receipts, reports and the fraud check.

Evidence.

  • The same switch (payment.Kind) appears in 5 files.
  • Each new method breaks one of the five places that were missed.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. Repeated switch on a type (OCP) with the behaviour scattered across classes (shotgun surgery).

Fix. One IPaymentMethod with Charge/Refund/Receipt; one class per method. New method = new class.

📖 Where to review: pages 3 and 8.

🩺 Case 5

A basic printer full of methods that throw

Symptom. The new basic printer class has ten lines of throw new NotSupportedException().

Evidence.

  • It implements IMachine with Print, Scan and Fax.
  • It only prints. Its fake in the tests needs three empty methods to run one check.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. A fat interface forces classes to carry methods they cannot use (Interface Segregation).

Fix. Split into IPrinter, IScanner, IFax; the basic printer implements only IPrinter.

📖 Where to review: page 5.

🩺 Case 6

Data from user A appears for user B

Symptom. Occasionally a customer sees another customer’s cart. Restarting the app “fixes” it.

Evidence.

  • CartCache is registered as a singleton and its constructor takes a scoped ICartRepository.
  • Scope validation is off in Production.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. A captive dependency: a singleton holds a scoped service for the app’s lifetime (DI lifetimes).

Fix. Make the cache scoped, or create a scope per use with IServiceScopeFactory; turn on scope validation.

📖 Where to review: page 7.

🩺 Case 7

Totals are calculated in three places

Symptom. Checkout, the invoice and the email each compute an order total and they disagree on rounding.

Evidence.

  • Each place loops over order.Lines and reads order.DiscountAmount.
  • Order itself has no methods.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. Behaviour sits far from the data it uses (Feature Envy), so it is duplicated.

Fix. Move Method: Order.Total(). Pin current totals with a test first.

📖 Where to review: page 8.

🩺 Case 8

Nobody can find where anything happens

Symptom. A new hire needs 9 file jumps to follow a one-field change. There are 40 interfaces and 40 classes.

Evidence.

  • Each interface has exactly one implementation, plus a factory returning it.
  • No test uses the interfaces.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. Presumptive abstraction (YAGNI): interfaces created by habit, not at real seams.

Fix. Collapse layers that only forward calls; keep interfaces at I/O, time and third-party boundaries; extract later when a second implementation appears.

📖 Where to review: page 11.

🩺 Case 9

The fake passes, production fails

Symptom. Bonus case. A FakeOrderRepository returns null for a missing order; the SQL one throws.

Evidence.

  • Tests pass; production crashes on missing ids.
  • There is no shared test for the two implementations.

Your move: which family is it, and what is your one change?

▾ Reveal the diagnosis

Diagnosis. The fake does not honour the contract (LSP in tests).

Fix. Decide the contract (null or exception), write one contract-test suite, run it against both.

📖 Where to review: pages 4 and 10.

▾ Go one level deeper (60 seconds)

Patterns. Cases 1, 4 and 7 all feel like “this file is a mess” but each has a different lever: extract a class, polymorphism, move a method. The evidence — git history, repeated switches, duplicated loops — tells you which.

Case 8 is the opposite problem. Too much design. Judgement means being able to remove structure as well as add it.

Make your own clinic. Take five real tickets from your team, strip the diagnosis and keep the symptom and evidence, then run it together.

🔥 Interview recall
  • Method: what hurts → who forces the change → which principle → smallest refactoring → prove it → or leave it.
  • One change, pinned test, small commit.
  • Families: responsibilities & change (1, 4, 7), contracts & interfaces (2, 5, 9), dependencies & testing (3, 6), judgement (8).
  • Similar symptoms, different causes: read the evidence before choosing a principle.
  • Fakes need contract tests; singletons must not hold scoped services; interfaces belong at real seams.
🎤 Say this in an interview
"I treat a design problem as an investigation: what hurts — changing, testing or understanding — who or what forces the change, and which principle the evidence points to. I pin behaviour with a test, make one small refactoring, and check the next change is cheaper. And sometimes the right answer is to simplify, not add structure."
⚠️ Common trap

Reaching for the same principle every time. Four of nine cases were not SRP.

Skipping the evidence. In every case one fact — a repeated switch, a captive registration, a constructor with 0 parameters — decided the diagnosis.
