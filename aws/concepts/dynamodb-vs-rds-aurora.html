DynamoDB and relational databases are not rivals — the difference is whether you know your questions up front.

Strengths and watch-outs of each, the DynamoDB partition key, consistency and capacity, and when to use both.

❓ "When would you pick DynamoDB, and when RDS or Aurora?"
👁 See it first
🎯 DynamoDB and relational databases are not rivals — DynamoDB wins when you know your questions and need steady speed at scale; relational wins when your questions keep changing.
▶️ Watch it happen: not rivals
📖 Why did this need to exist?

The Databases page gave a rule of thumb. This page goes one level deeper on the pair you will be asked about most. They differ in how you design (tables first or queries first), how you scale (up and out with replicas, or out by partitioning) and what you give up.

The single most important decision in DynamoDB is the partition key. A good one spreads load evenly. A poor one creates a hot partition and throttling. Understanding this one idea explains most of DynamoDB.

⚖️ Strengths and watch-outs

🛢️ RDS and Aurora

✅ Strengths
  • SQL, joins and multi-row transactions
  • Open-ended questions: new reports need no redesign
  • Multi-AZ, read replicas and (Aurora) shared storage across three zones
⚠️ Watch out for
  • Scale mostly by a bigger instance and read replicas
  • You manage connections and schema changes
  • RDS does not autoscale read replicas — add them yourself

⚡ DynamoDB

✅ Strengths
  • Steady low latency at huge scale
  • On-demand mode or provisioned capacity; no servers to size
  • Replicates for resilience and can span Regions (global tables)
⚠️ Watch out for
  • No joins; ad-hoc queries are hard
  • You must design the keys and indexes around known queries
  • A poor partition key causes hot partitions and throttling

▾ Go one level deeper (60 seconds) — capacity and consistency

Consistency. A read unit covers one strongly consistent read per second, or two eventually consistent reads per second, for an item up to 4 KB. Eventually consistent reads cost half as much, which is why AWS suggests them as a quick fix for throttled key ranges when freshness allows.

Capacity. Each partition is designed for about 3,000 read units and 1,000 write units per second; table throughput is limited by provisioned capacity or, in on-demand mode, by table-level limits. Adaptive capacity and automatic partition splitting often help hot partitions.

Indexes. Global secondary indexes can have their own key distribution; low-variety index keys commonly cause write throttling.

Relational scale. Read replicas are updated asynchronously; Multi-AZ gives a synchronous standby for failover; Aurora readers use asynchronous replication with failover priorities.

🔥 Interview recall
  • DynamoDB: design for your queries; partition key with many distinct values; hot partitions throttle.
  • Relational: design for the data, ask any question later; Multi-AZ for failover, replicas for reads.
  • DynamoDB reads are eventually consistent by default; strongly consistent reads cost double.
  • Write sharding spreads hot keys but complicates reads.
  • Use both when a system has key lookups and relational reporting.
🎤 Say this in an interview
"I choose DynamoDB when I know my access patterns and need steady speed at scale, and I spend my design effort on the partition key — many distinct values, evenly used — to avoid hot partitions. I choose RDS or Aurora when I need joins, transactions and questions that will change. Many systems use both, each for what it does best."
⚠️ Common trap

A low-variety partition key. Keys like a status or a date bucket that everyone writes to today create a hot partition; throughput that looked ample is wasted.

Second trap: using DynamoDB like a relational database. If you keep wishing for joins, the access patterns were not known — choose relational.

🛢️ Aurora

A relational engine with shared storage across three zones and many read replicas.

vs.
⚡ DynamoDB

A key-value / document database that scales by partitioning, designed around known queries.
