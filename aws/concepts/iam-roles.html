Deep dive · IAM · 🎭 Roles

A role is an identity with no password — something trusted steps into it, and AWS hands out temporary credentials.

Trust policies, permissions policies, instance profiles and STS: how roles really work, for EC2 and across accounts.

❓ "How does an EC2 instance or another account get permissions through an IAM role?"
👁 See it first
🎯 A role is an identity with no password: something trusted can step into it for a while, and AWS hands out temporary credentials that match its permissions.
▶️ Watch it happen: step into a role
📖 Why did this need to exist?

The IAM page said: use roles, not keys. This page shows how a role actually works, because the same few ideas explain EC2, Lambda, containers, pipelines and cross-account access.

A role has two policies with two different jobs: a trust policy (who may become this role) and a permissions policy (what the role may do). Learn that split and most "access denied" mysteries become easy to read.

🧰 The vocabulary

🤝 Trust policy

who may assume the role✅ Pick it when
  • Names the service or account allowed to use the role
  • For EC2 the trusted principal is the EC2 service
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Expecting it to grant any permission — it only lets someone in

📜 Permissions policy

what the role may do✅ Pick it when
  • The actions and resources, like any IAM policy
  • Keep it to least privilege
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Making it "admin" so the app stops failing

🏷️ Instance profile

the wrapper that carries a role to EC2✅ Pick it when
  • Holds exactly one role
  • The console creates it for you when you make the role for EC2
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Looking for it with Lambda or ECS — those attach roles directly

🎟️ AWS STS

the service that issues temporary credentials✅ Pick it when
  • AssumeRole returns an access key ID, a secret and a session token
  • Used for cross-account access and for people switching roles
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Calling it on EC2 to re-assume the instance's own role — not needed

🎬 Real-world scenarios
🖥️
An EC2 app reads from storage

No key on the disk.

→ Instance profile with a read-only role
⚡
A Lambda function writes to a table

It needs only that table.

→ An execution role
🏗️
A CI pipeline deploys to production

It lives in a tools account.

→ A cross-account role it assumes
🧑‍💻
An admin needs powerful rights for 30 minutes

Not all day.

→ Assume an admin role, then drop it
🚀
You launch an instance with a role

The launch fails with "not authorized to pass role".

→ Allow iam:PassRole for that role
🔄
You change the role on a running server

Is it instant?

→ Replace the instance profile; changes can take time
▾ Go one level deeper (60 seconds) — details that bite

Credentials renew themselves. For EC2, the SDK reads credentials from the instance metadata path for the role. They are temporary and AWS issues new ones at least five minutes before the old ones expire.

PassRole. The person or pipeline that launches an instance with a role needs permission to pass that role (iam:PassRole), not only permission to launch. Scope it to the one role and the one service it may be passed to.

Session permissions. The effective permissions of an assumed-role session are the overlap of the role's permissions and any session policy passed in, so a session can be narrower than the role.

Do not re-assume. A workload on EC2 does not need to call AssumeRole for the role already on the instance; it only needs it for other roles, or to add session tags or a session policy.

Changing a role on a running instance. Replace the instance profile rather than removing the role; removing a role from a profile is discouraged because the change can take a long time to take effect.

🔥 Interview recall
  • A role is assumed, not signed into: callers get temporary credentials from STS (access key ID, secret, session token).
  • Trust policy = who may assume. Permissions policy = what the role may do.
  • EC2 uses an instance profile (holds one role); the app reads rotating credentials from the instance metadata service.
  • Cross-account: the caller assumes a role in the other account; that role must trust the caller.
  • Launching with a role needs iam:PassRole, scoped to that role.
🎤 Say this in an interview
"A role has a trust policy that says who may assume it and a permissions policy that says what it can do. On EC2 an instance profile carries the role and the SDK reads temporary credentials from the metadata service, which AWS rotates. For cross-account access the caller assumes a role in the other account through STS and gets temporary keys. Launching with a role also needs iam:PassRole."
⚠️ Common trap

Mixing up the two policies. Adding a permission to the trust policy does nothing for what the role can do, and adding it to the permissions policy does nothing for who can assume it.

Second trap: trusting "the whole account" when you meant one role. A trust policy that names an entire account lets every identity there with the right permission assume the role. Name the narrowest principal you can.

🤝 Trust policy

Answers "who may become this role?" Attached to the role itself.

vs.
📜 Permissions policy

Answers "what may this role do?" Written like any other IAM policy.

▾ 💻 Tiny illustrative example 
# ask STS for temporary credentials for a role in another account
aws sts assume-role --role-arn arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/deploy --role-session-name pipeline

Illustrative shape only — the account ID is a placeholder, and the output holds an access key ID, a secret and a session token that expire.
