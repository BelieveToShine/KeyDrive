Concept 9 of 19 · ⚙️ Handling load

Scaling adds copies when load grows; resilience keeps you running when something fails — both come from never relying on just one of anything.

Auto Scaling, zones and Regions: how scaling decides, what each size of failure needs, and the coding habits that survive trouble.

❓ "How do you scale an AWS app and keep it available when a server, a zone or a Region fails?"
👁 See it first
🎯 Scaling adds copies when load grows. Resilience keeps the app running when a copy, a zone or a Region fails. They use the same trick: never rely on just one of anything.
📖 Why did this need to exist?

The last concept put a load balancer in front of several copies. Two questions follow at once. How many copies do you need? Too few and the app is slow at peak; too many and you pay for idle servers. What if a copy — or a whole zone — fails?

AWS answers the first with Auto Scaling: add copies when load rises, remove them when it falls. It answers the second with redundancy: copies in several Availability Zones and, for the worst day, a second Region. AWS's reliability guidance is to plan recovery before the failure, not after.

🧰 The tools

📈 EC2 Auto Scaling

add and remove copies automatically✅ Pick it when
  • Traffic that rises and falls
  • Target tracking, step scaling or a schedule
  • Spread across zones; replaces unhealthy instances
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • A single database server — it is not a pool of identical copies

🏢 Multiple Availability Zones

copies in separate zones✅ Pick it when
  • Survive the loss of one zone
  • Put each tier's copies in at least two zones
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Expecting zones to protect against deletion or corruption

📦 Service-level scaling

each service scales its own way✅ Pick it when
  • ECS services, DynamoDB capacity and Aurora readers have their own autoscaling
  • Lambda scales per event
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Assuming one setting scales everything

🛟 A second Region

for the biggest failures✅ Pick it when
  • The business cannot stop if a Region fails
  • Backups and failover for a real disaster
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • The cost is not justified by the app → zones are often enough

▾ Go one level deeper (60 seconds) — details that matter

Policy types. AWS describes target tracking, step and simple scaling as dynamic scaling types, and strongly recommends target tracking where it fits. It recommends against simple scaling policies and scaling cooldowns in favour of target tracking or step scaling. Mixing target tracking and step policies on the same metric can conflict.

Warm-up. The default instance warm-up sets how long after an instance is in service it waits before contributing metrics, so scaling does not react to instances that are not yet serving. AWS strongly recommends configuring it; it is not set by default.

Retry wisely. Retrying a transient failure with growing waits and some random jitter stops clients from all retrying at once. Add a circuit breaker so a truly dead dependency is not hammered, and make operations idempotent.

Zones are not backups. Spreading copies across zones keeps the app up; it does not recover deleted or corrupted data. That is the job of replication and backup (see the deep dive).

🔥 Interview recall
  • Scale up = bigger machine (ceiling). Scale out = more copies (Auto Scaling works this way).
  • Prefer target tracking: pick a metric target and AWS holds it. Step scaling for non-proportional metrics; scheduled scaling for known rushes.
  • Configure the default instance warm-up; avoid simple scaling and cooldowns.
  • Zones survive a zone failure; a second Region survives a Region failure. Zones do not replace backups.
  • Retry with back-off and jitter, add a circuit breaker, make operations idempotent.
🎤 Say this in an interview
"I design for failure at three sizes: a server, a zone and a Region. An Auto Scaling group spread across zones covers the first two, a second Region with backups covers the third. For load I use target tracking on a metric like CPU, scheduled scaling for known rushes and a configured warm-up. In code I retry with back-off and jitter, and keep operations idempotent."
⚠️ Common trap

Autoscale only helps if the app runs as many copies. If it keeps sessions or files on one server, a new copy will not know about them. Keep state outside the copies.

Second trap: "we are in two zones, so we have backups". Zones keep the app running; they do not protect against deletion or corruption. Redundancy and backup are different things.

📈 Scaling

Match the number of copies to the load, both ways. Mostly performance and cost.

🛟 Resilience

Keep working when something fails. Mostly availability and recovery.

▾ 💻 Tiny illustrative example 
# target tracking: keep average CPU of the group near a target value
aws autoscaling put-scaling-policy --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg --policy-name cpu-target \
    --policy-type TargetTrackingScaling --target-tracking-configuration file://target.json

Illustrative shape only — the group name is an example and target.json would hold the metric and target value you choose.
