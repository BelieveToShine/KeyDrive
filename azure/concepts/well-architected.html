📖 Why did this need to exist?

You now know the pieces: hierarchy, regions, identity, networks, compute, storage, databases, load balancing, scaling, caching, messaging, serverless, code for infrastructure, pipelines, secrets, security, signals and cost. Each one was a good answer to a real limit. But each one also has a price: another thing to run, pay for, secure or watch.

So the last skill is not another service. It is judgement: how to look at a whole design and ask whether it fits what the business needs. Microsoft packages that judgement in two frameworks. The Well-Architected Framework helps you review a workload. The Cloud Adoption Framework and its landing zone help you set up the environment that workloads live in.

🧰 The frameworks

⚖️ Well-Architected Framework

review one workload✅ Pick it when
  • Check a design against five pillars
  • Each pillar gives practices, risks and trade-offs
  • Record where you traded one pillar for another
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Using it as a checklist to tick — it is about decisions, not boxes

🧭 Cloud Adoption Framework

plan and govern the whole move to cloud✅ Pick it when
  • Strategy, planning, readiness and governance
  • Guidance for setting up and managing the environment
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Looking for service-level design details — that is the Architecture Center

🏔️ Landing zone

a ready, governed place for workloads✅ Pick it when
  • A platform landing zone with shared services (identity, connectivity, management)
  • Workload landing zones where application teams deploy
  • Policy and structure set up before the first app arrives
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • A one-off experiment — start small, grow into it

💡 Azure Advisor

recommendations on your real resources✅ Pick it when
  • Recommendations in cost, performance, reliability, security and operational excellence
  • A quick, practical starting point
⛔ Look elsewhere when
  • Treating it as a full architecture review — it looks at configuration and usage, not intent

🎬 Real-world trade-offs
🌍
"Let's go multi-region for safety."

Reliability goes up, and so do cost and complexity.

→ Is a zone-redundant design enough?
🔍
"Inspect and log every request."

Security and visibility go up; speed and cost can go down.

→ Decide what is worth inspecting.
⚡
"Always keep extra capacity warm."

Performance is smooth; you pay for idle capacity.

→ Autoscale, or reserve a baseline
🧑‍💻
"Let teams create what they like."

Speed is high; security and cost become unpredictable.

→ Policy guardrails in a landing zone
🔁
"Release five times a day."

Operations improve; risk must be managed with slots and checks.

→ CI/CD with a fast rollback
📝
"Why did we choose this?"

Nobody remembers the reasoning.

→ Write the trade-off down
▾ Go one level deeper (60 seconds) — how to use the frameworks in practice

Balance, not maximise. Microsoft says design decisions must be balanced across all pillars given the business requirements, and that adopting a recommendation may mean choosing against another pillar. For many production workloads, a zone-redundant deployment is a good balance; multiple regions raise reliability but add cost and complexity. Write the choice and the reason down.

Start from requirements. What must be true: how much downtime, how much data loss (RPO and RTO from concept 9), what compliance, what budget? The pillars are a way to check you did not forget a dimension.

Review regularly. A design that was right last year may not be now: traffic grows, prices and services change, teams change. Revisit with the framework and with Azure Advisor.

Landing zone terms move. The Cloud Adoption Framework has recently renamed "application landing zones" to "workload landing zones" and dropped the separate data-landing-zone categories; detailed architecture guidance now lives mainly in the Azure Architecture Center and the Well-Architected Framework. Check the current names.

🔥 Interview recall
  • Five pillars: Reliability, Security, Cost Optimization, Operational Excellence, Performance Efficiency.
  • The pillars trade off against each other; record where you chose one over another and why.
  • Well-Architected Framework reviews a workload; the Cloud Adoption Framework and landing zones set up the environment.
  • Landing zone = platform landing zone (shared services: identity, connectivity, management) + workload landing zones, under management groups and policy.
  • Azure Advisor gives recommendations across cost, performance, reliability, security and operational excellence.
🎤 Say this in an interview
"I review a design against the five Well-Architected pillars and accept that they trade off — for example a second region improves reliability but adds cost and complexity — so I write down the choices. For the environment I use a landing zone: management groups and policy at the top, a platform landing zone with shared identity, connectivity and management, and workload landing zones for each team. Then I review again as things change."
⚠️ Common trap

Treating the framework as a checklist. Ticking every recommendation is not the goal; some will cost more than the risk they remove. The value is in the explicit trade-offs.

Second trap: building the landing zone last. Guardrails added after a hundred workloads exist are far harder to apply than guardrails that every workload starts inside.
