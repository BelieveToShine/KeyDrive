❓ "What is the difference between authentication and authorization in Azure, and how does Azure RBAC work?"
👁 See it first
🎯 Identity answers two questions: who are you (Microsoft Entra ID) and what may you do (Azure RBAC). Access is always who + what + where.
▶️ Watch it happen: who + what + where
📖 Why did this need to exist?

Your resources now have a home and a place in the world. The next risk is people — and apps. Who may create a database, who may delete a resource group, and how does your own app get permission to read a file without anyone typing a password?

Azure splits this into two clear steps. Microsoft Entra ID proves who you are (authentication). Azure RBAC decides what you may do, and where (authorization). Keeping the two apart is what lets you give each person and each app exactly the access it needs — no more.

🔁 Two checks, in order
🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Who can be given a role?
👤 User

A person with an account in your Microsoft Entra tenant.

👥 Group

A set of users. Assign the role to the group once, then add or remove people — easier than assigning to each person.

🤖 Service principal

The identity of an application inside your tenant. Used when an app (or a pipeline) must act on its own.

🪪 Managed identity

A special service principal that Azure manages for an Azure resource — so your code needs no secret. See the deep dive below.

🎟️ The three roles you meet first
RoleWhat it can doTypical person
OwnerFull management of resources and can assign roles to othersA small number of trusted admins
ContributorFull management of resources, but cannot assign rolesDevelopers who build and run the app
ReaderView resources, change nothingAuditors, support, new team members
Permissions add up: if someone has Reader and Contributor on the same scope, the result is Contributor. And a role given at a higher scope is inherited by everything below it (see the resource hierarchy). Aim for least privilege: the smallest role at the lowest scope that lets the person or app do its job.
⚠️ Two kinds of roles — do not mix them up
🎬 Real-world scenarios
🧑‍💻
New developer joins

They must deploy the web app but never touch billing or other teams.

→ Contributor on their resource group
🔍
Auditor reviews Prod

Needs to see everything, change nothing.

→ Reader on the Prod subscription
👥
Ten people, same job

Assigning ten roles by hand is slow and error-prone.

→ One role to a group
🤖
Pipeline deploys the app

A script, not a person, needs access.

→ A service principal (or managed identity) with a role
🌐
Web app reads blobs

The code must read files without a password in its settings.

→ Managed identity + Blob data role
🛑
"Everyone is Owner"

It works today, until one mistake deletes everything.

→ Least privilege: lowest role, lowest scope

🔥 Interview recall
  • Authentication = who are you (Microsoft Entra ID → a token). Authorization = what may you do (Azure RBAC).
  • A role assignment = security principal (who) + role definition (what) + scope (where). Scopes: management group, subscription, resource group, resource.
  • Owner can assign roles; Contributor manages resources but cannot assign roles; Reader only views. Permissions add up and are inherited downward.
  • Microsoft Entra roles (the directory) and Azure roles (resources) are separate systems.
  • Prefer groups and managed identities; give least privilege at the lowest useful scope.
🎤 Say this in an interview
"In Azure, Microsoft Entra ID handles authentication — proving who a user or workload is and issuing a token — and Azure RBAC handles authorization. A role assignment combines a security principal, a role definition and a scope, and it is inherited down the hierarchy. I assign roles to groups, use managed identities for apps so there are no secrets, and follow least privilege at the narrowest scope that works."
⚠️ Common trap

Signing in is not the same as having access. A user who can sign in to the tenant, or a brand-new managed identity, can do nothing in Azure until a role is assigned. "I can log in but get an authorization error" almost always means a missing role at the right scope.

Second trap: Owner is powerful because it can hand out access. Giving Owner to "make things work" lets that person (or app) grant anyone anything. Reach for Contributor, or a narrower role, first.

▾ 💻 Tiny illustrative example 
# give a user the Reader role on one resource group (who + what + where)
az role assignment create \
    --assignee "asha@contoso.com" \
    --role "Reader" \
    --scope "/subscriptions/<subscription-id>/resourceGroups/rg-shop-prod"

Illustrative shape only — replace the placeholders; in real teams roles are usually assigned to groups, and defined in templates.
