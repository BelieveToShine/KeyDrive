Concept 5 of 19 · 🎓 Foundations

Transformers let each word weigh information from other relevant words directly, instead of waiting in line.

Neural networks gave us the building block. Transformers are the specific network shape that turned out to handle sequences — sentences, code, anything ordered — dramatically better than what came before.

❓ "What problem does 'attention' actually solve?"
👁 See it first
🎯 Transformers = a network built around attention, so each position can weigh information from other relevant positions directly.
📖 Why did this need to exist?

Earlier sequence models processed one word at a time, in order, carrying a running "memory" of everything seen so far forward to the next step. That works, but two things suffer: information from early words has to survive many hops to influence a much later word, weakening long-range relationships; and because each step depends on the previous one finishing, the whole sequence can't be processed in parallel.

The transformer's answer is attention: instead of relaying information hop by hop, every position computes, in one step, how much it should weigh other positions — near or far, it doesn't matter, so distance alone no longer weakens the signal. That removes the long-range weakening problem and lets the whole computation run in parallel, which is a large part of why transformers scale so well.

"Every position" isn't unconditional, though: which positions a given position is allowed to look at depends on the transformer. A causal language model — the shape behind most of today's text-generating LLMs — masks out future positions, so a word can only weigh itself and the words before it, not ones that haven't been generated yet. Encoder-style models built to understand a whole input at once (rather than generate it token by token) skip that mask and let every position see every other position.

▾ Go one level deeper (60 seconds)

"Attention" is a weighting mechanism: for a given word, it computes a relevance score against every other word in the sequence, turns those scores into weights, and blends the other words' information according to those weights. A word usually ends up paying most attention to a handful of genuinely relevant words, not equally to all of them — the mechanism can reach any position, but it still has to learn which ones matter.

This "attend to everything, weigh what matters" building block, stacked in layers like any other neural network, is what almost every modern large language model is built from.

  • Attention lets a position connect directly to other positions it's allowed to see, not just neighbors — causal models restrict that to earlier positions only.
  • It solves two problems at once: long-range relationships weakening, and step-by-step processing blocking parallelism.

🎤 Say this in an interview
"Transformers replace step-by-step sequence processing with attention, where each position computes a relevance-weighted view of the other positions it's allowed to see, in one step — causal language models mask out future positions so generation stays left-to-right. That fixes the long-range-dependency weakness of earlier sequential models and lets the computation run in parallel, which is a big part of why transformers scale so well."
⚠️ Common trap

Attention doesn't mean "reads the whole input equally." A position can reach every position it's allowed to see directly, but the learned weights usually concentrate on a few genuinely relevant ones. "Direct access" and "pays attention to everything the same amount" are not the same claim — only the first one is true, and even "direct access" is scoped by masking in causal models, not automatically the whole input.

▾ 💻 Tiny illustrative example 
# Attention, at the shape level (not the real math)
words = ["The", "trophy", "didn't", "fit"]

# for one word, compute a relevance score against every word
scores  = [relevance(words[3], w) for w in words]   # incl. itself
weights = softmax(scores)                          # turn scores into weights that sum to 1
context = sum(w * embed(word) for w, word in zip(weights, words))

Illustrative shape only — real attention runs this for every word, across many layers, with learned projections rather than a hand-written relevance().
